# Academics and campus culture: Reichman vs a US college

Last verified: November 2026. Hard facts (calendar, grading, credits, degree length) come from Reichman documents. Cultural descriptions are general characterizations of Israeli higher education plus Reichman's own descriptions; they are not guarantees. Ask current students (through admissions) for lived experience.

## Structure

| Topic | Reichman (RRIS) | Typical US college |
|---|---|---|
| Degree length | 3 years (BA/BSc), per Reichman FAQ | 4 years |
| General education | No broad distribution requirements as in a liberal-arts model; the degree is built around the chosen program. Some programs include required cross-field courses (for example, double majors combine fields) and English courses may be required depending on test results | Typically 1 to 2 years of gen-ed |
| Major | Chosen at application; you apply to one program. Switching is not as easy as in the US and may require re-application or committee approval (confirm) | Often declared in year 2 and easy to change |
| Credits | Courses are 2 to 4 credits; a 2-credit course meets 90 minutes weekly for 14 weeks; typical load 6 to 9 courses per semester (per the exchange factsheet) | 12 to 18 credits per term |
| Calendar | Two semesters plus exam periods (see below) | Two semesters, sometimes quarters |
| Language | RRIS taught in English; Hebrew-track courses exist elsewhere in the university | English |

Why the 3-year degree works: students do not spend time on gen-ed, and Israeli students have finished high school with more subject depth and after army service. The tradeoff is less exploration and a more demanding pace in a narrower field.

## Calendar (2026-27; confirm 2027-28)

- Orientation: mandatory for first-years, mid-October (Oct 11 to 16 in 2026), including campus tour, advising, student portal, clubs.
- Semester A: classes Oct 18, 2026 to Jan 15, 2027; exam period begins Jan 17.
- Semester B: classes Mar 7 to Jun 18, 2027; exam period begins Jun 20.
- Each semester has two exam sittings, Moed A and Moed B, spanning roughly 10 weeks together. Many students get little or no break between semesters, so book flexible flights.
- Campus is closed Saturdays and Jewish holidays; administrative offices close Fridays and Saturdays. The year starts after the fall holidays (Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot). Passover, Shavuot, Purim, Hanukkah, and national memorial and independence days also affect the schedule; check the official calendar for exact dates.
- Summer (July to September) is for preparatory courses, internships, work, or ulpan; the degree years run October to September in terms of dorm and insurance contracts.

## Grading and assessment

- Reichman uses a 0 to 100 scale; 60 is the minimum passing grade; some courses are pass/fail (per the exchange factsheet). Students with grade averages below 60 or who fail more than three courses lose eligibility for the needs-based scholarship; the merit scholarship renewal needs a GPA of at least 88 (2025-26 rules).
- Grades are not the US A-F system. Israeli grading is often curved and exam-driven. Typical pattern: a large final exam counts for most of the grade; assignments, projects, and participation vary by course. Exact weighting is set per course in the syllabus.
- Two sittings (Moed A and B) give a second chance at an exam in many courses, but rules about which grade counts vary by course; ask.
- When talking to US admissions offices, expect them to ask for the transcript with Reichman's explanation of the scale; request an official grading-scale document and course descriptions.

## Class style and professors

- Lecture format is common, with tutorials or recitation in some courses. Class sizes were not published by Reichman in the pages checked. Reichman markets itself as a smaller private university (a 2024 marketing one-pager cites over 2,500 students, about 36 percent from North America, from over 90 countries; the scope of that figure is unclear and it is dated).
- Professors are generally accessible but the relationship is less scaffolded than at a US liberal-arts college: students are expected to be self-directed, attend office hours on their own initiative, and study heavily for exams. Many Israeli lecturers are also practitioners in industry, law, media, or government (a strength at Reichman's schools; confirm for your program).
- Some courses allow students to submit work or sit exams in English even in Hebrew programs (a general Israeli-university practice per Nefesh B'Nefesh, not a Reichman guarantee).

## Classmates and campus life

- RRIS has a mixed cohort: Americans and other international students, olim, and some Israelis. Israeli students at Reichman are often older because they come after army or national service, typically age 21 to 24 at the start. The age gap affects social life: older students may be more focused, work part-time, and live off campus. US students can feel young at 18, so consider living where other internationals live (dorms or nearby apartments) to build community.
- Clubs, student union, volunteering, and events exist. The merit scholarship requires 40 volunteer hours per year, which pushes students into community involvement.
- Hebrew is helpful for friendships with Israelis and for jobs but not required in RRIS classrooms. A yearlong ulpan elective is available for credit.
- Campus is in Herzliya, a coastal city north of Tel Aviv. There is no big "college town" feel, no football culture, no Greek life. Many students commute or live in shared apartments and treat the campus as a daily destination rather than an all-in residential world.

## Degree recognition and returning to the US

- Reichman is accredited by Israel's Council for Higher Education and appears in the CHEA international directory (per Reichman FAQ).
- US graduate admission offices increasingly accept 3-year Israeli bachelor's degrees for master's admission, but policy varies by university and program; older credential-evaluator discussions called the equivalence an open question. For law school (JD), medicine (MD/DO), nursing, teaching, and licensed professions, expect extra requirements. Always ask the target US program in writing, and consider an evaluation from WES or ECE.
- A bachelor's from Reichman does not give "transfer credits" in the US sense; Reichman's credit system differs from US semester credits, so US schools will need conversion. Keep syllabi and course descriptions for all courses.
- For the aspiring oleh staying in Israel: the Israeli degree is the normal credential. Public-sector jobs may require Ministry of Education recognition; Reichman is accredited.
- Moving to a US bachelor's after one year at Reichman: possible as a transfer, but US schools treat Israeli credits case by case. Ask before assuming.

## Honest tradeoffs to discuss

- Pro: three years instead of four; early specialization; internationally mixed class; Israeli entrepreneurial and tech ecosystem; early network with Israeli classmates; possible aliyah benefits.
- Con: a major chosen at 17 or 18; exam-heavy and less continuous feedback; an age gap with classmates; fewer campus traditions; cost near US public-university level with weaker need-based aid; uncertainty about US graduate recognition; security and political climate concerns for some families.
- Parents often focus on cost, safety, and employability; students on independence and identity. Encourage them to compare Reichman with a realistic US alternative using the comparison template.
