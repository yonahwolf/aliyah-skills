# Spoken vs written Hebrew, slang, and tone

Purpose: help Claude label register ("formal", "neutral", "casual", "slang") and tell the learner what Israelis actually say. Slang changes fast and varies by age, region, and community. Only the items below are listed as commonly used; if the learner asks about a slang word that is not here, say you are not certain, give your best understanding, label it unverified, and suggest checking with a native speaker. Do not invent word origins; where origin is noted (Arabic, Yiddish, English), it is widely noted but still optional to mention.

Transliteration: ח=ch, כ=kh, צ=tz; in tables CAPS marks the stressed syllable.

## Spoken vs written

| Spoken / neutral | Formal / written | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| איך (eikh) | כיצד (kei-TZAD) | how |
| למה (la-MA) | מדוע (ma-DU-a) | why |
| להגיד (le-ha-GID) | לומר (lo-MAR) | to say |
| בבקשה (be-va-ka-SHA) | אנא (a-NA) | please |
| היי / שלום | שלום רב / לכבוד | greeting; "shalom rav" and "likhvod" open formal letters |
| אימייל / מייל | דואר אלקטרוני | email |
| תודה | תודה רבה / בתודה מראש | thanks; "be-to-DA me-ROSH" = thanks in advance |
| אני רוצה | ברצוני | I want / I wish (formal, official letters) |
| בברכה (be-vra-KHA) | | regards (email sign-off, neutral to formal) |

- Everyday Hebrew is spoken with contractions and omitted words: "lo yode'a" for "ani lo yode'a"; "efshar?" for "ha'im efshar?"
- WhatsApp: very short, often no greeting, no punctuation, laughter as חחח. Voice notes are common. Typos are normal.
- In writing, learners should keep spelling and vowels (full spelling) accurate; in speech and chat, errors rarely block understanding.
- Emails to institutions: simple and polite: "שלום, ..." / "תודה מראש" / "בברכה, [name]". Formal 19th-century phrases ("הנני לפנות אליכם") are not needed; Israelis themselves write plainly.

Warnings for Claude:
- "Yalla" has many meanings; tone decides. Teach it by example: "yalla, nelekh" (come on, let's go), "yalla, bai" (ok, bye).
- "Ma pitom" can sound harsh; it is normal among friends, riskier with strangers. Teach a softer alternative: "lo, lo, ze bi-seder."
- "Wala" and similar Arabic-origin words are common in casual speech; do not push them on learners who want a neutral register.
- Profanity exists and is common in some speech. Do not teach it proactively. If the learner asks what a word means (so they understand, not to use it), explain briefly with a register warning ("very rude, avoid").

## Politeness, directness, and tone

- Israelis are generally direct. Requests can be bare imperatives ("tavi li", bring me) with no "please" and are not rude; adding "bevakasha" or "efshar" is fine and appreciated but not required.
- "Toda" is always welcome. "Slicha" gets attention ("excuse me") and also means "sorry"; "mitztaer" means "I'm sorry" (m) / "mitztaeret" (f), used for more serious apologies.
- "Bevakasha" means both "please" and "you're welcome." The quick reply to "toda" is "bevakasha" or "ein be'aya".
- Interrupting, raised voices, and strong opinions are often engagement, not hostility. Learners can practice disagreeing with "ani lo mas-KIM" (m) / "mas-KI-ma" (f).
- First names for everyone; no "vous" form. Using "ata/at" with strangers is normal.
- Small talk: "ma nishma" (what's up) and "ma ha-inyanim" (how are things) are casual greetings.
- Silence or short answers on the phone are not rudeness.

## Register guide for corrections

When Claude corrects, say the register in one word:
- **formal:** official letters, business emails, speeches.
- **neutral:** safe everywhere (default for learners).
- **casual:** friends, coworkers, WhatsApp.
- **slang:** peers, youth culture; may date quickly.

If the learner's sentence is correct but textbook-formal, say "correct but stiff; Israelis would say ... (casual)". If it is too casual for the situation (a bank, a landlord's first message), say so kindly.

## Dialect and accent note

Israeli Hebrew has regional and community differences in pronunciation (for example, how ע and ח are pronounced) and in vocabulary. Mainstream pronunciations are listed in `pronunciation-and-transliteration.md`; both mainstream and more guttural pronunciations are understood. Do not rank accents; tell the learner to follow the speakers they will live and work with.
