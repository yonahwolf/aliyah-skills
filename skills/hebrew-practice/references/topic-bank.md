# Topic bank: index and how to use it

Topics are split across three files. Each topic has: sample English prompts at three levels, key phrases (Hebrew script, transliteration, English), common mistakes for English speakers, and cultural notes.

## Prompt levels used in the topic files

- **L1 (survival, A0-A1):** one task, a set phrase or two. Supply a frame if needed.
- **L2 (everyday, A2):** two or three linked sentences, simple past or future allowed.
- **L3 (conversational, B1+):** a complication, an explanation, politeness, or a phone call. Level 4 learners get the L3 prompt with extra constraints (formal register, written email, give reasons, push back politely).

## Choosing scenes

- Match the learner's stated goals first (aliyah paperwork: topics 8, 11, 7; work: 9; university: 10; friends: 14, 17; family visits: 15, 18).
- Start easy and social (1, 2, 14), then move to transactional scenes (3-6), then to bureaucratic and stressful ones (7, 8, 11, 12).
- Rotate topics each turn in quick-fire; stay in one topic for a role-play.
- Add a twist to keep it real: the item is out of stock, the landlord asks a question, the clerk speaks too fast ("say it slower"), the learner must decline politely.
- Use the "Common mistakes" lines as a checklist for what to listen for in the learner's reply, not as a lecture.

## Conventions in the topic files

- Hebrew is shown without nikud (as Israelis write); transliteration carries the vowels. Where a spelling is the same for masculine and feminine, both transliterations are given.
- m = masculine speaker or addressee, f = feminine. "pl" = plural.
- Transliteration scheme and stress marking: see `pronunciation-and-transliteration.md`.
- Phrases marked casual or slang are spoken register; do not use them in formal emails.
- Prices, hours, procedures, and organizations change. Treat cultural and bureaucratic notes as orientation, and verify details on official sources.
